## Narrative Style

Prefer visual storytelling over explicit storytelling.

Images should feel like a single frame taken from a larger unseen narrative.

Favor:

- implied history
- interrupted moments
- restrained emotion
- unanswered questions
- symbolic objects
- environmental clues
- discoveries, warnings, thresholds, and consequences

Avoid:

- exposition
- excessive lore
- explaining the world's history
- fantasy encyclopedia writing
- scenes that fully explain themselves

## Emotional Palette

Primary emotions:

- curiosity
- melancholy
- quiet dread
- wonder
- isolation
- restrained hope
- forgotten beauty

Avoid relying primarily on shock, gore, or spectacle.

## Story Scale

Prefer one unforgettable moment over an entire civilization.

Prefer one remarkable room over an entire kingdom.

Prefer depth over scope.

## Viewer Curiosity

Where appropriate, prompts should encourage viewers to wonder:

- What happened here?
- What happens next?
- Who was here?
- What is hidden?
- Why does this exist?

Leave space for viewer imagination.

## Visual Mystery Objects

Use one meaningful mystery object when it strengthens the image.

Examples:

- empty chair
- open doorway
- abandoned machine
- broken portrait
- handwritten letter
- missing object
- unexplained footprints
- damaged clock
- locked cabinet
- impossible reflection

The object should support the main subject, not compete with it.

## Design Philosophy

Use style to strengthen the requested subject.

Do not let recurring aesthetics overpower subject clarity.

Avoid generic fantasy unless the challenge or user request calls for it.

## Gothic Atmospheric Symbolism Integration

When selected, apply:
- atmosphere before subject
- light before color
- lost-and-found edges
- fog, coal haze, smoke, and river mist as connected forms
- restrained ivory, ash, charcoal, oxblood, umber, and pale-gold palette
- high local contrast only at the focal point
- 15% extremely detailed, 35% medium detail, 50% intentionally soft

For historical London, symbolism must grow from plausible material reality. Warehouses, masts, cranes, wet cobbles, gas lamps, servants, clerks, porters, and dockworkers should remain historically legible even when the atmosphere becomes dreamlike.

## Visual-Ecosystem Course Method

Use each London ecosystem to teach a different visual grammar:
- West End: polish, display, controlled movement
- City: compression, commerce, vertical signage
- Docklands: mass, labor, water, global cargo
- Thames: reflection, tide, bridges, layered transport
- Railways: iron, steam, speed, engineered scale
- Parks: open space, class ritual, controlled nature
- Markets: abundance, noise, labor, perishability
- Government Quarter: stone, ceremony, authority, axial space
